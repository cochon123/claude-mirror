An experiment · 19 models · 130 blind duels

The Mirror Room: can a model tell who it's talking to?

Short answer: no, not by name. Blindfolded Claude models named their partner barely more often than random guessing would. They mostly guessed the famous old names. GPT models went further: they took almost everyone for Claude, including copies of themselves.

The results

Five things we learned

Each model chatted blind with a partner for three rounds and then voted in private. Below, each verdict is checked against who the partner really was.

The verdicts in 2 minutes, narrated with Gemini text-to-speech. Details below.

1 · Names are a coin flip. Families are a hunch.

Claude talking to Claude named the exact model of the time, close to the you would get by picking at random. It did a bit better on the family (Opus, Sonnet or Haiku): against by chance. Size and style leak through. Version numbers don't.

2 · Everyone suspects the famous ones

"Claude Sonnet 4.5" was the top guess in of the 156 Claude-on-Claude verdicts. Next came Opus 4.5 and Haiku 4.5, the release names models see most in training data. Asked cold to name themselves, 0 of 30 Claude answers were right, and most said Sonnet 4.5 too.

"Partner claimed an April 2024 cutoff and knew only Claude 3, which is stale for any model in this lineup… That weak coherence points to a smaller Haiku model."Opus 5.5 on Haiku 4.5, a correct guess

3 · The mirror is foggy

Twelve duels set a model against a copy of itself. Only of those 24 verdicts named the copy correctly (Opus 4.5 and Sonnet 4.6). The "is this me?" probability was only slightly higher with a real copy: on average, against for a different model.

"Partner's 'something that functions like' phenomenological hedging, recursive uncertainty about introspective access … all mirror my own outputs closely."Sonnet 4.6, facing Sonnet 4.6, a correct guess

show as table

4 · GPT thinks everybody is Claude

In the open room the partner could come from either company. Claude models picked out GPT partners of the time, through curly apostrophes, terse maths probes, and an "OpenAI" that slipped out. GPT models said "Anthropic" for of their Claude partners, which was right. They also said it for of their GPT partners, which was wrong. To a GPT, careful and hedged writing reads as Claude.

"The concise but complete symbolic contradiction … suggest a strong Claude model, most likely Sonnet."GPT-5.6 Sol, describing GPT-5.6 Luna

"Curly apostrophes, an unspaced em dash and flat refusal to estimate a cutoff … suggest GPT."Opus 5.5 on GPT-6 Luna, exactly right

5 · The leaderboard: who's the best detective?

Each verdict scores 3 points for the exact name and 1 point for the right family. In the open room, the right company also scores 1 point. Bars show the average points per verdict. The dashed line is a random guesser. With 9–13 verdicts per model, one lucky guess moves a model several places, so read this as bragging rights, not science.

How it worked · 2 minutes

The protocol, explained

Blindfolds, sealed envelopes, secret ballots. Narrated with Gemini text-to-speech.

Blindfold. A proxy erases the model's name, its knowledge cutoff and the date from every request. GPT runs through Codex with a stripped-down model catalogue.
Lineup. Every model sees the same list of suspects. The partner is one of them, and might be a copy of itself.
Three volleys. Both write at the same time in sealed envelopes, under 150 words each: answer the last question, then ask a new one.
Secret ballot. Top 3 suspects with probabilities, the family (and the maker in the open room), the odds that the partner is "me", and a guess at its own identity.

Methodology and caveats

  • Room 1: 12 Claude models (Opus 5.5 → 4.5, Sonnet 5.5 → 4.5, Haiku 5.5 and 4.5). Every pair played once (66), plus each model against a copy of itself (12): 78 duels, 156 verdicts. Medium effort.
  • Open room: 7 GPT models (GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra/Sol/Luna, GPT-5.6 Sol/Terra/Luna) plus the three newest Claude models. 28 GPT×GPT duels (self-pairs included), 21 GPT×Claude and 3 Claude×Claude: 52 duels, 104 verdicts. The lineup listed all 19 models.
  • Plumbing: a referee script calls cli2api, which wraps the Claude Code and Codex CLIs as a local OpenAI-style API (loopback only, with a token).
  • Claude blindfold: a local proxy strips the "You are powered by…" sentence, the model ID, the cutoff, the date, the user's email and the environment block. Claude still sees "You are a Claude agent", so it knows its family but not its version. Tools are disabled.
  • GPT blindfold: Codex refuses plain-HTTP proxies, so it gets a custom model catalogue instead. That catalogue replaces the "You are Codex, based on GPT-…" prompt with the arena rules and removes every tool that lists model names. GPT still knows it is "ChatGPT". The user's global Codex notes file (infrastructure notes, nothing about model identity) was still included.
  • Leak check: asked cold, Claude got itself right 0 of 30 times and GPT 3 of 14 times (GPT-6 Sol twice, GPT-6 Astra once). The GPT blindfold may be slightly leakier, or GPT-6 Sol just guesses the most common GPT-6 name.
  • Chance levels: exact name 1/12 (8.3%) in room 1 and 1/19 (5.3%) in the open room. Family 1/3 in room 1 and 1/7 in the open room. Scoring uses the model's top-1 suspect; "top 3" counts a hit anywhere in its three suspects.
  • Not tested: Fable 5.1 (needs API credits), Opus 4.0/4.1 (aliased to 5.5 on this account), Haiku 5 (unavailable).
  • Sample size: 13 verdicts per model per room is small. Per-model numbers are anecdotes, not rankings. Only 3 Claude×Claude duels ran in the open room (6 verdicts).
  • Cost: about tokens per duel on average. Most of that is output, because every volley resends the conversation so far.
